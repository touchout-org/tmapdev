A Brief History of TMAP

The original Tactile Map Automated Production (TMAP) was created in 2003 at the Smith-Kettlewell Eye Research Institute by Dr. Joshua Miele as part of his post-doctoral research. The goal was to build an accessible, automated system allowing blind and visually-impaired people to independently generate tactile street maps of any U.S. location and evaluate its impact on wayfinding and independent travel. Against his better judgement, Miele wrote the original TMAP software himself, using geospatial data from TIGER Line data, and later Tele Atlas through Sendero.

TMAP was unveiled publicly at the 2004 CSUN conference where Miele embossed many individualized street maps on request in the exhibit hall. Later that year, Smith-Kettlewell launched the TMAP web site that allowed anyone with a graphics-capable braille embosser to enter an address through an accessible web interface and immediately download a well-formatted embossable tactile map with braille labels. For the first time, simple, well-formatted, tactile street maps of any location in the U.S. were freely available and accessible to anyone with a braille embosser.

To expand the information that could be included on a single map, Miele opened a fruitful collaboration with Touch Graphics to build Talking TMAP – an audio/tactile street map experience built on the Talking Tactile Tablet.

In 2007 the San Francisco LightHouse began using TMAP to produce street maps for its students to support independent travel. Over its lifetime from 2004 to 2013, Smith-Kettlewell's original TMAP produced thousands of tactile street maps for independent blind travelers all over the U.S.

But in 2013 the over-taxed and obsolete TMAP infrastructure finally gave up the ghost, leaving us without the easy access to tactile street maps that had become so empowering. It would be 5 years before Smith-Kettlewell could secure NIDILRR funding to rebuild TMAP with modern tools, using open street data and a more sustainable delivery model.

TMAP was reborn in 2018 as a collaborative handoff from Smith-Kettlewell to the San Francisco LightHouse, finally locating the service at a direct-service institution, with significant and immediate improvements to availability, reliability, and adding a number of popular new features.

As of this writing (August, 2026), the San Francisco LightHouse for the Blind and Visually Impaired continues to offer free TMAPs for download, as well as a low-cost service to obtain tactile street maps by mail.

Credits

TMAP for Dot Pad is a creation of Josh Miele, with welcome input from a global community of tactile map lovers. TMAP for Dot Pad's source code is released under the open-source MIT License.

Funding and support for the original development of TMAP was provided by the Smith-Kettlewell Eye Research Institute and the National Institute on Disability, Independent Living, and Rehabilitation Research (NIDILRR).
